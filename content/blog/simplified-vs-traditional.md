When you start learning Chinese, someone will ask: "Are you learning simplified or traditional?" It's one of the first decisions — and it feels bigger than it is. Here's what you actually need to know.

---

## The Short Answer

**Learning simplified?** You'll communicate with 1.4 billion people in mainland China, Singapore, and Malaysia. You'll be able to read every modern Chinese novel, newspaper, and app.

**Learning traditional?** You'll be able to read in Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Macau, plus access thousands of years of classical texts in their original form.

**The reassuring truth:** If you learn one well, switching to the other takes weeks, not years. About two-thirds of characters are identical in both scripts.

---

## What's the Difference?

Traditional Chinese characters (繁體字 fántǐzì) have been in use for over a thousand years. When the People's Republic of China launched literacy reforms in the 1950s and 1960s, they created simplified characters (简体字 jiǎntǐzì) — reducing stroke counts to make writing faster and reading easier.

Some simplifications are dramatic:

| Simplified | Traditional | Pinyin | Meaning |
|-----------|------------|--------|---------|
| 爱 | 愛 | ài | love |
| 书 | 書 | shū | book |
| 马 | 馬 | mǎ | horse |
| 国 | 國 | guó | country |
| 飞 | 飛 | fēi | fly |
| 门 | 門 | mén | door |
| 龙 | 龍 | lóng | dragon |
| 电 | 電 | diàn | electricity |

Others were left completely unchanged:

| Character | Pinyin | Meaning |
|-----------|--------|---------|
| 人 | rén | person |
| 大 | dà | big |
| 中 | zhōng | middle |
| 山 | shān | mountain |
| 水 | shuǐ | water |
| 来 | lái | come |

About 2,235 characters were simplified. Of those, roughly 500 are changed substantially enough that a simplified-only reader would struggle to recognise them in traditional script without study.

---

## How Were Characters Simplified?

The simplification wasn't arbitrary — it used several systematic methods:

**1. Shortening existing cursive forms.** Calligraphers had already developed faster handwritten versions of many characters. Simplification often just standardised the cursive form.

> 书 comes from a cursive shorthand of 書 that calligraphers had used for centuries.

**2. Using phonetic components alone.** Some characters dropped their semantic component and kept only the phonetic.

> 从 (cóng, from) simplified 從 by removing the repeated element.

**3. Merging homophone characters.** Some traditional characters that sounded the same were merged into one simplified form.

> 发 simplified two different characters: 發 (fā, to send/emit) and 髮 (fà, hair). In traditional, these are distinct. In simplified, context tells them apart.

**4. Analogical simplification.** When a radical was simplified, all characters using that radical were updated.

> When 言 (speech) was simplified to 讠, every character with the speech radical changed: 说→说, 话→话, 请→请, etc.

## Where Each Script Is Used

| Region | Script | Notes |
|--------|--------|-------|
| Mainland China | Simplified | Official government script since 1956 |
| Taiwan | Traditional | Official script; also called Mandarin or 國語 |
| Hong Kong | Traditional | Combined with Cantonese |
| Macau | Traditional | Combined with Cantonese and Portuguese |
| Singapore | Simplified | Official; also used in Malaysia |
| Overseas communities | Mixed | Depends on emigration era and origin |

Chinese diasporas tend to use traditional characters if their families emigrated before the 1950s (Hong Kong, Taiwan, older US/Canada/Australia communities) and simplified if they emigrated from the mainland more recently.

---

## Does It Affect Learning Difficulty?

Traditional characters have more strokes — but stroke count is less important than it sounds. You recognise characters as whole units, not by counting strokes. Most learners don't notice the difference in reading speed once they're past the beginner stage.

Where traditional can be harder:
- **Writing by hand** — more strokes takes longer
- **Initial visual complexity** — some traditional characters look intimidating

Where traditional can be *easier*:
- **Phonetic components are clearer.** Simplified sometimes collapsed the phonetic component, making it harder to guess pronunciation from structure.
- **Semantic components are clearer.** Some simplifications obscured the radical, making meaning harder to infer.

> 爱 (simplified) vs 愛 (traditional): The traditional form contains 心 (heart) in the middle — you can *see* the emotion. The simplified version removed it.

---

## The "Crossover" Question

Once you're comfortable in one script, you can typically read the other with a few weeks of study. The characters you'll have trouble with are the ~500 that changed substantially — everything else either looks the same or the simplification is obvious.

Many learners who start with simplified eventually pick up traditional for specific purposes — reading Taiwanese media, classical texts, or historical documents. The reverse is also common: traditional readers who work in mainland China pick up simplified for professional use.

---

## Which Should You Choose?

**Choose simplified if:**
- Your primary goal is communicating with mainland Chinese speakers
- You want to read modern Chinese apps, social media, news
- You're learning for business with China
- You're going to take the HSK exam (HSK uses simplified)

**Choose traditional if:**
- You're focused on Taiwan, Hong Kong, or Macau
- You want to read classical literature or historical texts
- You're learning Cantonese (traditional is used for written Cantonese)
- Your heritage community uses traditional script

**Either one if:**
- You just want to start and aren't sure yet — pick simplified (more learner resources available) and know you can always add traditional later

## The Practical Reality

For spoken Mandarin, the script choice doesn't matter at all. The pronunciation, grammar, and vocabulary are the same (with minor regional differences). You're choosing which *written* form you'll recognise and produce.

HanziDict includes both simplified and traditional for every entry — you can toggle between them in the settings. When you look up a character, you'll see both forms so you always know what you're working with.

Start with whichever script matches your goals. Get to 1,000 characters in that script, then decide if you need the other one. The answer will be obvious by then.
