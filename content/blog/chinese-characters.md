In Chinese, instead of an alphabet, you have **characters** (汉字 *hànzì*). Each character represents a word, a concept, or sometimes just a grammatical particle. Most Chinese words are made of one or two characters, occasionally three.

The number that gets thrown around is 3,000–4,000 characters to read a newspaper. That sounds daunting — but here's the reassuring truth: **you can get by with around 400–500 characters**. That's still a lot, but it's a very different prospect from 4,000. And unlike memorising random shapes, characters follow patterns that make each new one easier than the last.

Characters are also part of the *essence* of Chinese — the culture, the history, the way of thinking. Understanding them, even partially, gives you access to a writing system that has been in use for several thousand years.

---

## What Does a Character Look Like?

Let's start with two characters that are good to know:

> **汉** *hàn* — refers to the Han Chinese people, and to the Han Dynasty, considered the golden age of Chinese history

> **字** *zì* — means "character" or "word"

Put them together: **汉字** *hànzì* — literally "Han characters," the standard word for Chinese characters.

Add **语** *yǔ* (language) after 汉: **汉语** *hànyǔ* — literally "the language of the Han," meaning Chinese. (In everyday speech, most people say **中文** *zhōngwén* instead — 汉语 is slightly more formal and academic.)

## Radicals: The Building Blocks

Chinese characters are made of smaller components called **radicals** (部首 *bùshǒu*), also called "keys." There are 214 traditional radicals. A radical can be an entire character on its own, or just a part of one.

Radicals have two main functions:
- They hint at **meaning** — the semantic category of a character
- They hint at **pronunciation** — when used as a phonetic component

Characters "tell stories," as people often say — though the stories don't always go where you expect them to.

---

## A Radical in Action: 青 qīng

Here's one of the most useful examples to understand how radicals work.

**青** *qīng* is a character that describes a colour somewhere between blue and green — like turquoise. It's also a **radical**, and it appears inside several other characters:

| Character | Pinyin | Meaning |
|-----------|--------|---------|
| 清 | qīng | clear, pure |
| 晴 | qíng | sunny, clear (of weather) |
| 请 | qǐng | please; to invite; to ask |
| 情 | qíng | emotion, feeling |

All four are pronounced *qing* — the tones differ, and so do the meanings. What they share is 青 acting as the **phonetic component**: it tells you roughly how to say the character.

But they also each carry a **semantic radical** on the left that hints at meaning:

**清 qīng** (clear, pure):
The left side is **氵** — the water radical, a simplified form of 水 *shuǐ* (water). Imagine water the colour of turquoise: clear and pure.

**晴 qíng** (sunny):
The left side is **日** *rì* — the sun. The turquoise colour of 青, combined with the sun, suggests a blue, sunny sky.

**请 qǐng** (please, to invite):
The left side is **讠** — the speech radical, from 言 *yán* (speech, words). Any character with this radical relates to communication. Other examples: 说 *shuō* (speak), 语 *yǔ* (language), 诗 *shī* (poem).

**情 qíng** (emotion):
The left side is **忄** — the heart radical, from 心 *xīn* (heart). The heart drives emotion. Other characters with this radical: 快 *kuài* (fast, happy), 怕 *pà* (to fear), 想 *xiǎng* (to want, to think, to miss).

This is the key insight: **most Chinese characters combine a meaning hint and a pronunciation hint**. Once you know 青, you can make educated guesses about any new character that contains it. And once you know the common radicals, you can decode the semantic category of hundreds of characters at a glance.

## Two Writing Systems

There's one more thing you need to know about Chinese characters: there are two versions.

**Simplified characters** (简体字 *jiǎntǐzì*) are used in mainland China and Singapore. They were standardised in the 1950s when only about 20% of the Chinese population was literate — simplifying the characters was part of a literacy drive.

**Traditional characters** (繁體字 *fántǐzì*) are still used in Hong Kong, Taiwan, and Macau. They're closer to the historical forms.

Take the word **汉字** itself:

| Simplified | Traditional | Pinyin |
|-----------|------------|--------|
| 汉字 | 漢字 | hànzì |

The traditional character 漢 has **14 strokes**. The simplified 汉 has **5 strokes**. The second character, 字, wasn't simplified — it was already considered simple enough.

Not every character was changed. Many are identical in both scripts. And characters that were simplified often kept the logic intact — the simplifications weren't random. Which system you learn depends on where you plan to use Chinese (see our [guide to simplified vs traditional](/blog/simplified-vs-traditional)).

## Stroke Order

Every character is drawn with strokes — individual pen movements. There's a prescribed order for these strokes, and it matters.

The general rules:
- **Top before bottom**
- **Left before right**
- **Horizontal strokes before vertical ones that cross them**
- **Outside before inside** (but the sealing stroke of an enclosure comes last)

"But what difference does it make if I write the strokes in a different order — the character looks the same in the end?"

This is a common question. The answer: strokes written in the wrong order tend to look slightly off, and the more you write, the more obvious the difference becomes. Correct stroke order is also faster — it was developed ergonomically over centuries. And for handwriting recognition on phones and tablets, stroke order matters for the input to work correctly.

The rule is: **build the habit from the first character**. It's much harder to unlearn later. HanziDict shows animated stroke order for every character — you can watch the correct sequence before you practise.

## Does Handwriting Still Matter?

Many learners decide they only need to *read* Chinese, not write it by hand — since everything is typed on phones and computers these days. Even native Chinese speakers forget how to write characters they know perfectly well how to read, because they type everything.

But here's the case for learning to write, at least early on:

**Writing reinforces reading.** The motor memory of tracing a character builds the visual memory of recognising it. Characters you've written tend to stick in a way that characters you've only seen don't.

**You can't always rely on your phone.** If you're in China and your phone dies, or you need to communicate something quickly by hand, knowing a few hundred characters can get you out of situations that knowledge of pinyin alone won't.

**It teaches you to see structure.** When you write characters, you start noticing radicals naturally — which parts are shared between characters, which components carry meaning, which ones carry sound. That awareness makes reading faster.

You don't need to practise writing every character you learn. But for the ones that keep slipping away, tracing them a few times often locks them in.

## Where to Start

The most efficient path through characters:

1. **Learn the common radicals first.** Fifty radicals covers the vast majority of everyday characters. Once you know 氵, 讠, 日, and 心, you can decode the semantic category of new characters on sight.

2. **Learn characters in words.** 好 *hǎo* is more useful as 你好 *nǐhǎo* (hello) than as an isolated character. Context gives you hooks to remember.

3. **Use spaced repetition.** The forgetting curve for characters is steep. HanziDict's flashcard system shows you characters just before you'd forget them — a few minutes a day keeps hundreds of characters fresh.

4. **Start reading as soon as you can.** Menus, signs, subtitles — real-world reading trains your pattern recognition in ways that structured study can't replicate.

The first 500 characters are the hardest. After that, you have enough building blocks that every new character is an extension of something you already know.
