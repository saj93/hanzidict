There are roughly 50,000 Chinese characters. You need about 2,000 to read a newspaper. The good news: most of those 2,000 are combinations of a much smaller set of building blocks — and once you understand how those blocks work, learning new characters gets dramatically easier.

---

## The Six Types of Characters

Chinese characters aren't random drawings. Linguists classify them into six types based on how they were originally formed. Two types account for the vast majority of characters you'll actually encounter.

### 1. Pictographs (象形字 xiàngxíngzì)

The oldest characters — direct drawings of the things they represent.

| Character | Meaning | What it looked like originally |
|-----------|---------|-------------------------------|
| 日 rì | sun | a circle with a dot in the centre |
| 月 yuè | moon | a crescent moon |
| 山 shān | mountain | three peaks |
| 木 mù | tree | trunk with roots and branches |
| 水 shuǐ | water | flowing ripples |

Modern characters look more abstract than the originals because they've been squared off and standardised over millennia of writing. But the resemblance is still there if you know what to look for.

### 2. Simple Ideographs (指事字 zhǐshìzì)

Abstract ideas represented through simple visual additions.

> **上** shàng (above) — a line above a base line  
> **下** xià (below) — a line below a base line  
> **一 二 三** yī èr sān — literally one, two, three strokes

### 3. Compound Ideographs (会意字 huìyìzì)

Two or more pictographs combined to suggest a new meaning.

> **明** míng (bright) = 日 sun + 月 moon  
> **休** xiū (rest) = 人 person + 木 tree → a person leaning against a tree  
> **森** sēn (forest) = 木 + 木 + 木 → three trees

These are genuinely logical — not arbitrary. When you encounter 明 you can reason: *something involving the sun and the moon — it must relate to light or brightness*.

### 4. Phono-semantic Compounds (形声字 xíngshēngzì)

This is the big one. **Over 80% of Chinese characters** fall into this category.

Each character has two parts:
- A **semantic component** (radical) — hints at meaning
- A **phonetic component** — hints at pronunciation

Take the character 请 *qǐng* (to invite/please):

- 讠(言) — the speech radical, meaning the character relates to speaking or communication
- 青 *qīng* — the phonetic component; characters using 青 often rhyme with *qīng*

Once you know 青 *qīng*, you can make educated guesses about:
- 清 qīng (clear, of water) — 氵 water radical + 青
- 情 qíng (emotion) — 忄 heart radical + 青
- 请 qǐng (please, invite) — 讠 speech radical + 青
- 晴 qíng (sunny) — 日 sun radical + 青

The tones don't always match perfectly (this is ancient Chinese phonology at work), but the rhyme and consonant usually do. This pattern is enormously useful for decoding unfamiliar characters.

## Radicals (部首 bùshǒu)

Radicals are the semantic building blocks — the part of a character that hints at its meaning category. Chinese dictionaries are traditionally organised by radical.

Here are the most useful radicals to learn first:

| Radical | Meaning | Example characters |
|---------|---------|-------------------|
| 氵(水) | water | 海 hǎi sea, 河 hé river, 洗 xǐ wash |
| 讠(言) | speech/language | 说 shuō speak, 话 huà words, 请 qǐng please |
| 口 | mouth | 吃 chī eat, 喝 hē drink, 叫 jiào call |
| 人(亻) | person | 你 nǐ you, 他 tā he, 做 zuò do |
| 木 | wood/tree | 树 shù tree, 椅 yǐ chair, 桌 zhuō table |
| 女 | woman | 妈 mā mum, 姐 jiě older sister, 她 tā she |
| 心(忄) | heart/emotion | 想 xiǎng think, 怕 pà fear, 爱 ài love |
| 手(扌) | hand/action | 打 dǎ hit, 拿 ná hold, 找 zhǎo find |
| 日 | sun/day | 明 míng bright, 时 shí time, 晚 wǎn evening |
| 草(艹) | plant | 花 huā flower, 草 cǎo grass, 茶 chá tea |

You don't need to memorise all 214 traditional radicals. The 50 most common ones cover the vast majority of everyday characters.

## Strokes and Stroke Order

Every character is made up of strokes — the individual marks made in a single pen movement. Stroke order is the prescribed sequence for writing each stroke.

**Why does stroke order matter?**

1. **Consistency** — everyone who learns standard stroke order produces recognisable characters even when writing quickly
2. **Speed** — proper stroke order is ergonomically optimised; it's faster once habitual
3. **Digital recognition** — handwriting input on phones uses stroke order to disambiguate characters

**The basic rules:**

- Left before right
- Top before bottom
- Horizontal strokes before vertical ones that cross them
- Outside before inside (for enclosed shapes), but the sealing stroke comes last

A character like 国 (country) is: outer box sides → inside content → bottom-sealing stroke.

## Simplified vs Traditional

Modern Chinese exists in two written forms:

**Simplified** (简体 jiǎntǐ): Used in mainland China, Singapore, and Malaysia. Standardised in the 1950s–60s to increase literacy. Some characters were dramatically simplified; others stayed the same.

**Traditional** (繁體 fántǐ): Used in Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Macau. Closer to the historical forms — many radicals and phonetic components are more visually apparent.

| Simplified | Traditional | Pinyin | Meaning |
|-----------|------------|--------|---------|
| 爱 | 愛 | ài | love |
| 书 | 書 | shū | book |
| 语 | 語 | yǔ | language |
| 国 | 國 | guó | country |
| 马 | 馬 | mǎ | horse |

About 2,000 characters were simplified. Many common characters are identical in both scripts. If you learn one, reading the other is much easier than starting from scratch.

## The Practical Learning Order

Don't try to learn characters in isolation. The most efficient approach:

**1. Learn radicals first.** A few weeks on the top 50 radicals pays dividends for years. Once you recognise 氵, 讠, and 口, you can decode the semantic category of hundreds of new characters on sight.

**2. Learn characters in words.** 好 (*hǎo*, good) is more useful as 你好 (*nǐ hǎo*, hello) and 好吃 (*hǎo chī*, delicious) than as an isolated character. Context gives you retrieval cues.

**3. Use spaced repetition.** The forgetting curve is brutal with characters. HanziDict's flashcard system surfaces characters just before you'd forget them — a few minutes a day is enough to keep 1,000 characters solid.

**4. Write by hand, at least early on.** The motor memory reinforces the visual memory. You don't need to write everything — but for characters you keep forgetting, handwriting them a few times often locks them in.

**5. Read as soon as possible.** Even reading simple texts — restaurant menus, signs, subtitles — trains your pattern recognition in a way that pure study can't replicate.

## The One Thing to Remember

Chinese characters are not a wall of randomly-shaped symbols. They are a systematic writing system where most characters combine a *meaning hint* and a *pronunciation hint*. The more characters you know, the easier it gets to guess new ones — because you start recognising the phonetic components you already know.

The first 500 characters are the hardest. After that, you have enough building blocks that every new character is an extension of something familiar.
