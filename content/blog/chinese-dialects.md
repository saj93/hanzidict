When you say you're learning Chinese, you probably mean Mandarin. But if you told a linguist you were learning "Chinese," they might ask: which one?

Chinese is not a single language. It's a family of languages — or dialects, depending on who you ask — spoken across China, Taiwan, and large overseas communities worldwide. They share the same writing system and a common cultural history, but many of them are mutually unintelligible. A Cantonese speaker from Hong Kong and a Mandarin speaker from Beijing cannot understand each other in speech.

This article won't change what you're learning. But understanding the bigger picture changes how you see the language.

---

## Dialects or Languages?

This question is more political than linguistic.

In linguistics, the standard test for whether two speech varieties are the same language is mutual intelligibility — can speakers understand each other without prior exposure? By that test, Mandarin and Cantonese are clearly different languages. A native Cantonese speaker cannot understand Mandarin without learning it, and vice versa.

But in China, they're officially called 方言 *fāngyán* — dialects. The political logic: one country, one language, many regional varieties. This framing has been contested, particularly in Hong Kong and Taiwan, where many speakers consider Cantonese and Taiwanese Hokkien to be languages in their own right.

A useful way to think about it: Chinese is a family of related languages, the way Romance languages (French, Spanish, Italian) are related. They come from a common ancestor, share a writing system, but diverged significantly in speech over centuries.

## The Main Chinese Language Families

![Geographic distribution of Chinese language families across China](/blog/chinese-languages-map.svg)

**Mandarin (普通话 Pǔtōnghuà)**

The largest group by far, spoken natively by around 920 million people across northern and southwestern China. Standard Mandarin, called Putonghua on the mainland and Guoyu (國語) in Taiwan, is based on the Beijing dialect and serves as the national standard language. It has 4 tones plus a neutral tone.

Mandarin itself has significant regional variation. The Beijing dialect is the basis for the standard but has its own distinctive features — heavy use of the retroflex *r* sound (儿化 érhuà) and a particular cadence that sounds very different from southern Mandarin. Northeastern Mandarin (东北话 Dōngběihuà), spoken across Heilongjiang, Jilin, and Liaoning, is often considered the closest to standard Mandarin. Sichuan and Chongqing dialects are part of the Southwestern Mandarin group — widely spoken and quite distinctive, with different tones and vocabulary.

**Cantonese / Yue (粤语 Yuèyǔ)**

Spoken in Guangdong province, Hong Kong, and Macao, as well as large overseas Chinese communities. Many of the earliest Chinese immigrant communities came from Guangdong, which is why Cantonese historically dominated in overseas communities.

The standard greeting 你好 sounds very different here: *nei5 hou2* in Jyutping — though in everyday Hong Kong speech you'll more often hear *lei5 hou2* (a well-documented shift where *nei* becomes *lei*).

One variety worth knowing about: **Taishanese** (台山话 Tāishānhuà), spoken in Taishan county in western Guangdong. It's part of the Yue family — related to but clearly distinct from Standard Cantonese, with different tones, vocabulary, and enough pronunciation differences that speakers can't always understand each other. Historically, Taishanese was the dominant language of overseas Chinese communities in North America: for much of the 19th and early 20th century, Chinese immigrants to the US and Canada came predominantly from the Taishan region, not from Guangzhou or Hong Kong.

**Wu (吴语 Wúyǔ)**

Spoken in Shanghai, Zhejiang, and parts of Jiangsu. Shanghainese (上海话 Shànghǎihuà) is the most widely known Wu variety. Wu languages are known for their complex tone systems and softer consonants. In Shanghainese, hello is roughly *nóng hó*.

Another notable Wu variety is 温州话 Wēnzhōuhuà, spoken in Wenzhou city in southern Zhejiang. It's so distinct that even other Wu speakers struggle to understand it — sometimes called the most impenetrable dialect in China.

**Min (闽语 Mǐnyǔ)**

The most geographically scattered family. Min varieties include Hokkien (spoken in Fujian, Taiwan, Singapore, Malaysia, and the Philippines), Teochew, and Hainanese. Taiwanese Hokkien, often simply called Taiwanese (台語 *Táiyǔ*), is widely spoken in Taiwan alongside Mandarin. In Hokkien: hello is *lí hó* (你好).

**Hakka (客家话 Kèjiāhuà)**

Spoken by the Hakka people, scattered across Guangdong, Fujian, Jiangxi, and large diaspora communities. Hakka speakers have a strong cultural identity tied to their language. In Hakka: hello is *ngî hó* (你好, where *ngî* = you).

**Gan and Xiang**

Two smaller families spoken in Jiangxi and Hunan respectively. Less studied outside China but together accounting for tens of millions of speakers.

## The Language Family Tree

![Family tree showing the Sinitic branch and its main varieties](/blog/chinese-language-tree.svg)

All Chinese varieties belong to the Sinitic branch of Sino-Tibetan — the same family that includes Tibetan and Burmese, though those are completely unintelligible with Chinese. The Sinitic languages share a writing system but diverged in speech over thousands of years.

## Saying Hello in Different Languages

Here's how 你好 sounds across a few varieties — same characters, completely different sounds:

| Language | Written | Romanization | Notes |
|----------|---------|--------------|-------|
| Mandarin | 你好 | Nǐ hǎo | The most widely known |
| Cantonese | 你好 | nei5 hou2 (Jyutping) | Colloquially *lei5 hou2* in Hong Kong |
| Shanghainese | 你好 | Nóng hó | Very different from Mandarin |
| Hokkien | 你好 | Lí hó | Common in Taiwan and SE Asia |

A good illustration of how the writing system unifies what the spoken languages divide.

## Non-Chinese Languages of China

China is home to 55 officially recognized ethnic minorities, many with their own languages that are completely unrelated to Chinese.

**Zhuang (壮语 Zhuāngyǔ)** is the most spoken minority language, with around 16 million speakers in Guangxi. It belongs to the Tai-Kadai family — more closely related to Thai and Lao than to Chinese.

**Tibetan (藏语 Zàngyǔ)** is spoken across the Tibetan Plateau. It belongs to the Sino-Tibetan family (as does Chinese), but the two are not mutually intelligible and have been separate for thousands of years.

**Uyghur (维吾尔语 Wéiwú'ěryǔ)** is spoken by around 10 million people in Xinjiang. It's a Turkic language written in a modified Arabic script, with no structural resemblance to Chinese.

**Mongolian (蒙古语 Měnggǔyǔ)** is spoken in Inner Mongolia and belongs to the Mongolic family.

For a Mandarin learner, these languages are mostly background knowledge. But they explain why China's linguistic reality is far more complex than "everyone speaks Chinese."

## Why Overseas Chinese May Not Speak Mandarin

This surprises many learners: you meet someone of Chinese heritage, try out your Mandarin, and they don't understand. There's a historical reason for it.

Most early Chinese emigration — to Southeast Asia, the Americas, Europe, and Australia — happened in the 19th and early 20th centuries, primarily from Guangdong and Fujian provinces. These emigrants spoke Cantonese, Hokkien, Teochew, Hakka, and other southern varieties. Mandarin was not widely spoken in those regions at the time.

As a result, older Chinese communities in cities like San Francisco, London, Singapore, or Kuala Lumpur were built around Cantonese or Hokkien. Many families maintained those languages across generations while having little exposure to Mandarin. More recent immigration has brought more Mandarin speakers — but in long-established communities, especially among older generations, Mandarin may well be a second language, not the first.

## The Writing System as Common Ground

One thing that makes Chinese unique among language families: the writing system bridges the spoken differences.

Because Chinese characters represent meaning rather than sound, a Cantonese speaker and a Mandarin speaker can read the same newspaper and understand it — even though they would pronounce every word completely differently. The character 我 means "I/me" in all varieties, even if a Cantonese speaker says *ngo5* and a Mandarin speaker says *wǒ*.

For you as a Mandarin learner, this means the characters you're learning give you access to a written tradition shared across all Chinese varieties — and across two thousand years of written history.
