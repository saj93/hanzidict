In English, asking a question usually means rearranging the word order: *"You are tired"* → *"Are you tired?"*. Chinese doesn't do this. In Mandarin, the word order stays the same — you signal a question by adding a particle at the end, using a question word, or repeating the verb.

This makes Chinese questions easier than you'd expect. Once you have the patterns, you can ask anything.

---

## Pattern 1: The 吗 Particle (Yes/No Questions)

Add 吗 *ma* to the end of any statement and it becomes a yes/no question. Word order stays identical.

> 你很累。*Nǐ hěn lèi.* — You are tired.  
> 你很累**吗**？*Nǐ hěn lèi ma?* — Are you tired?

> 他是学生。*Tā shì xuésheng.* — He is a student.  
> 他是学生**吗**？*Tā shì xuésheng ma?* — Is he a student?

> 你喜欢咖啡。*Nǐ xǐhuān kāfēi.* — You like coffee.  
> 你喜欢咖啡**吗**？*Nǐ xǐhuān kāfēi ma?* — Do you like coffee?

**Answering:** Chinese doesn't have universal yes/no words equivalent to English "yes" and "no." You echo the main verb:

> 你是老师吗？— Are you a teacher?  
> **是**，我是老师。— Yes, I am.  
> **不是**，我是学生。— No, I'm a student.

> 你喜欢辣的吗？— Do you like spicy food?  
> **喜欢**。— (Yes,) I do.  
> **不喜欢**。— I don't.

是 *shì* and 对 *duì* (correct) can function as loose equivalents of "yes" in casual contexts. 不 *bù* and 没 *méi* are the main negative words.

---

## Pattern 2: A-Not-A Questions

An alternative to 吗 — repeat the verb in positive and negative form to offer a choice.

> 你**是不是**学生？*Nǐ shì bu shì xuésheng?* — Are you a student (or not)?  
> 你**喜不喜欢**咖啡？*Nǐ xǐ bu xǐhuān kāfēi?* — Do you like coffee (or not)?  
> 你**去不去**？*Nǐ qù bu qù?* — Are you going (or not)?

With the verb 有 *yǒu* (to have/exist):
> 你**有没有**时间？*Nǐ yǒu méi yǒu shíjiān?* — Do you have time?

A-not-A questions sound slightly more natural than 吗 questions in speech. They also can't carry an assumed answer — where a 吗 question can sound like you're fishing for confirmation, A-not-A is genuinely open.

---

## Pattern 3: The 呢 Particle (Bounce-Back Questions)

呢 *ne* is used to ask "what about...?" or turn a question back to someone. It's always short — it presupposes context.

> 我很好。你呢？— I'm fine. And you?  
> 他去北京，你呢？— He's going to Beijing. What about you?  
> 我喜欢绿色，你呢？— I like green. What about you?

呢 can also signal a searching question — "where is...?", "what happened to...?":
> 我的手机呢？— Where's my phone?  
> 他人呢？— Where is he?

---

## Pattern 4: The 吧 Particle (Confirmation Seeking)

吧 *ba* turns a statement into a soft question seeking agreement — like "...right?" or "...I assume?".

> 你是英国人**吧**？— You're British, right?  
> 你累了**吧**？— You must be tired.  
> 这个很贵**吧**？— This is expensive, isn't it?

吧 implies you have a belief and are seeking confirmation. Using 吗 instead would mean you have no idea.

## Pattern 5: Question Words

Chinese uses question words the same way English does — but they stay in the same position they'd occupy as regular nouns or verbs. There's no fronting ("what are you doing?" in English moves "what" to the front; in Chinese it stays where the answer would be).

| Question word | Meaning | Position |
|--------------|---------|----------|
| 什么 shénme | what | in place of the noun |
| 谁 shéi / shuí | who | subject or object position |
| 哪 nǎ | which | before classifier + noun |
| 哪里 / 哪儿 nǎlǐ / nǎr | where | same position as place |
| 什么时候 shénme shíhou | when | same position as time |
| 怎么 zěnme | how | before the verb |
| 怎么样 zěnmeyàng | how about, how is it | end of sentence |
| 为什么 wèishénme | why | before the verb phrase |
| 多少 duōshǎo | how many/much | before noun (no classifier needed) |
| 几 jǐ | how many (small number) | before classifier + noun |

### Examples

**什么 shénme — what:**
> 你在做**什么**？*Nǐ zài zuò shénme?* — What are you doing?  
> 你叫**什么**名字？*Nǐ jiào shénme míngzi?* — What's your name?  
> 这是**什么**？*Zhè shì shénme?* — What is this?

**谁 shéi — who:**
> **谁**是你的老师？— Who is your teacher?  
> 你在等**谁**？— Who are you waiting for?

**哪里 nǎlǐ — where:**
> 你住在**哪里**？— Where do you live?  
> 洗手间在**哪里**？— Where is the bathroom?

**什么时候 shénme shíhou — when:**
> 你**什么时候**来？— When are you coming?  
> 他**什么时候**回国？— When is he going back to his country?

**怎么 zěnme — how:**
> 这个字**怎么**写？— How do you write this character?  
> 这个**怎么**说？— How do you say this?  
> 你**怎么**来的？— How did you get here?

**为什么 wèishénme — why:**
> 你**为什么**学中文？— Why are you learning Chinese?  
> 他**为什么**不来？— Why isn't he coming?

**多少 duōshǎo / 几 jǐ — how many:**
> 这个多少钱？— How much does this cost?  
> 你有**几**个兄弟？— How many brothers do you have?  
> 你要**几**杯？— How many cups do you want?

几 implies a small number (under 10). Use 多少 when you're uncertain of the scale.

---

## Indirect Questions

Chinese indirect questions (embedded in another sentence) use the same question word — no word order change needed.

> 我不知道他在哪里。— I don't know where he is.  
> 我想知道你喜欢什么。— I'd like to know what you like.  
> 你知道他什么时候来吗？— Do you know when he's coming?

Note that the outer sentence in the last example uses 吗 because the whole sentence is a direct question — but the embedded "when" clause stays in statement order.

## The Pattern to Internalise

Chinese questions are simpler than English ones because **word order never changes**. The question type is signalled by:

1. **A particle at the end** — 吗 (yes/no), 吧 (seeking confirmation), 呢 (what about you / where is it)
2. **An A-not-A structure** — verb + 不 + verb
3. **A question word in position** — what/who/where/when/why stays where the answer would go

Once you have these patterns, you can form any question in Chinese. The grammar is already simpler than English — you just need to retrain the instinct to rearrange word order.
