In English, turning a statement into a question usually means rearranging the word order: *"You speak Chinese"* → *"Do you speak Chinese?"* Chinese doesn't do this. The word order stays exactly the same — you signal a question by adding a **particle at the end**, using a **question word**, or **repeating the verb**.

This makes Chinese questions simpler than you'd expect. There are essentially three tools to learn.

---

## The Question Particles: 吗, 呢, 吧

Chinese has three main question particles, each used in a different context. All three go at the **end of the sentence** and carry a neutral tone.

---

### 吗 ma — Yes/No Questions

吗 *ma* is the basic question particle. Add it to any statement and it becomes a yes/no question. Word order doesn't change.

> 他是英国人。*Tā shì yīngguó rén.* — He is British.  
> 他是英国人**吗**？*Tā shì yīngguó rén ma?* — Is he British?

> 她会说中文。*Tā huì shuō zhōngwén.* — She speaks Chinese.  
> 她会说中文**吗**？*Tā huì shuō zhōngwén ma?* — Does she speak Chinese?

> 他们想去北京。*Tāmen xiǎng qù Běijīng.* — They want to go to Beijing.  
> 他们想去北京**吗**？*Tāmen xiǎng qù Běijīng ma?* — Do they want to go to Beijing?

Think of 吗 as the Chinese equivalent of "do you...?" or "is it...?" — but placed at the end rather than the beginning. It turns a declarative sentence into a question without changing anything else.

**Answering:** Chinese doesn't have universal yes/no words. Echo the verb instead.

> 你是老师吗？*Nǐ shì lǎoshī ma?* — Are you a teacher?  
> **是**，我是老师。*Shì, wǒ shì lǎoshī.* — Yes, I am.  
> **不是**，我是学生。*Bú shì, wǒ shì xuésheng.* — No, I'm a student.

---

### 呢 ne — "What About...?" / Follow-Up Questions

呢 *ne* is quite different from 吗. It's used for **open questions** — bouncing back to someone, asking for more information, or following up on what was just said.

> 我很好，你**呢**？*Wǒ hěn hǎo, nǐ ne?* — I'm fine, and you?  
> 他去北京，你**呢**？*Tā qù Běijīng, nǐ ne?* — He's going to Beijing. What about you?

呢 can also be added after another question word to give it extra emphasis — like adding "then" or "so" in English:

> 他为什么不想去**呢**？*Tā wèishénme bù xiǎng qù ne?* — Why doesn't he want to go, then?

You could drop the 呢 here and the sentence still works. But 呢 adds a slight sense of pressing the question.

呢 is also used in a short "where is...?" sense when something is missing:

> 我的手机**呢**？*Wǒ de shǒujī ne?* — Where's my phone?  
> 他人**呢**？*Tā rén ne?* — Where is he?

---

### 吧 ba — "...Right?" / Seeking Confirmation

吧 *ba* signals that you already have a belief and you're looking for confirmation — like ending a sentence in English with "right?" or "isn't it?"

> 你是英国人**吧**？*Nǐ shì yīngguó rén ba?* — You're British, right?  
> 你是英国人，对**吧**？*Nǐ shì yīngguó rén, duì ba?* — You're British, aren't you?  
> 我们晚上再聊，好**吧**？*Wǒmen wǎnshàng zài liáo, hǎo ba?* — We'll talk again tonight, OK?

Where 吗 is neutral ("I genuinely don't know"), 吧 implies you have an assumption you're checking.

---

## Pattern 2: The A-Not-A Question (Verb + 不 + Verb)

An alternative to 吗 — repeat the verb in positive and negative form, side by side:

> 你是**不是**英国人？*Nǐ shì bu shì yīngguó rén?* — Are you British (or not)?  
> 你**想不想**去？*Nǐ xiǎng bu xiǎng qù?* — Do you want to go (or not)?  
> 你**喜不喜欢**辣的？*Nǐ xǐ bu xǐhuān là de?* — Do you like spicy food?

Notice that for two-character verbs, only the first character is repeated before 不:  
你喜欢吗 → 你**喜不喜欢** (not 你喜欢不喜欢)

With the verb 有 *yǒu* (to have), use 没 *méi* instead of 不:

> 你**有没有**时间？*Nǐ yǒu méi yǒu shíjiān?* — Do you have time?

A-not-A questions sound very natural in spoken Chinese. Unlike 吗 questions, which can sometimes suggest you're fishing for a particular answer, A-not-A is genuinely open.

---

## Pattern 3: 没有 méiyǒu — "...Or Not Yet?"

Adding 没(有) *méi(yǒu)* to the end of a statement creates a question meaning "...or not yet?"

> 你吃饭了没（有）？*Nǐ chīfàn le méi(yǒu)?* — Have you eaten yet (or not)?  
> 你回家了没有？*Nǐ huí jiā le méiyǒu?* — Have you gone home yet?

This structure is especially common with past or completed actions.

## Pattern 4: Question Words

Chinese question words stay in **the same position in the sentence as the answer would**. There's no fronting like in English ("What are you doing?" moves "what" to the start). In Chinese, the question word stays where the answer belongs.

| Question word | Pinyin | Meaning |
|--------------|--------|---------|
| 什么 | shénme | what |
| 谁 | shéi / shuí | who |
| 哪里 / 哪儿 | nǎlǐ / nǎr | where |
| 什么时候 | shénme shíhou | when |
| 怎么 | zěnme | how |
| 怎么样 | zěnmeyàng | how is it / how about |
| 为什么 | wèishénme | why |
| 哪[个] | nǎ [ge] | which |
| 多少 | duōshǎo | how many / how much (10+) |
| 几[个] | jǐ [ge] | how many (under 10) |

### 什么 shénme — What

> 你喜欢**什么**？*Nǐ xǐhuān shénme?* — What do you like?  
> 你想吃**什么**？*Nǐ xiǎng chī shénme?* — What do you want to eat?  
> 这是**什么**？*Zhè shì shénme?* — What is this?

### 为什么 wèishénme — Why

Literally: 为 *wèi* (for, in order to) + 什么 *shénme* (what). Comes before the verb.

> 你**为什么**想学中文？*Nǐ wèishénme xiǎng xué zhōngwén?* — Why do you want to learn Chinese?  
> 他**为什么**不来？*Tā wèishénme bù lái?* — Why isn't he coming?

### 哪里 nǎlǐ — Where

> 你在**哪里**？*Nǐ zài nǎlǐ?* — Where are you?  
> 你是**哪里**人？*Nǐ shì nǎlǐ rén?* — Where are you from?  
> 洗手间 / 厕所在**哪里**？*Xǐshǒujiān / cèsuǒ zài nǎlǐ?* — Where is the bathroom?

### 怎么 zěnme — How

> 这个字**怎么**写？*Zhège zì zěnme xiě?* — How do you write this character?  
> 你**怎么**来的？*Nǐ zěnme lái de?* — How did you get here?  
> 你最近**怎么样**？*Nǐ zuìjìn zěnme yàng?* — How are you lately?

### 什么时候 shénme shíhou — When

> 你**什么时候**去上海？*Nǐ shénme shíhou qù Shànghǎi?* — When are you going to Shanghai?  
> 她**什么时候**下班？*Tā shénme shíhou xiàbān?* — When does she finish work?

### 哪[个] nǎ — Which

> 你在中国**哪个**城市？*Nǐ zài Zhōngguó nǎge chéngshì?* — Which city are you in in China?  
> 你觉得**哪一个**最好？*Nǐ juéde nǎ yīge zuì hǎo?* — Which one do you think is best?

### 谁 shéi — Who

> 你是**谁**？*Nǐ shì shéi?* — Who are you?  
> 你想跟**谁**去吃饭？*Nǐ xiǎng gēn shéi qù chīfàn?* — Who do you want to go eat with?

Note that "with whom" keeps 跟 *gēn* (with) before 谁, in the same position it would be in the answer.

### 多少 duōshǎo / 几 jǐ — How Many

Both mean "how many/much" but in different contexts:

**几** *jǐ* — for small quantities you expect to be under 10. Can also mean "a few":
> 你有**几个**孩子？*Nǐ yǒu jǐge háizi?* — How many children do you have?  
> 我有**几个**。*Wǒ yǒu jǐge.* — I have a few.

**多少** *duōshǎo* — for larger or unknown quantities, and always for prices:
> 你班上有**多少**学生？*Nǐ bān shàng yǒu duōshǎo xuésheng?* — How many students are in your class?  
> 这个**多少钱**？*Zhège duōshǎo qián?* — How much does this cost?

## The Pattern to Internalise

Chinese questions are simpler than English ones because **word order never changes**. The question type is signalled by:

1. **A particle at the end** — 吗 (yes/no), 呢 (what about you / where is it), 吧 (seeking confirmation)
2. **Verb + 不/没 + verb** — the A-not-A structure
3. **没有 at the end** — "...or not yet?"
4. **A question word in place of the answer** — it stays exactly where the answer would go

Once these patterns feel natural, you can form any question in Chinese. The hardest part is unlearning the English instinct to rearrange words.
