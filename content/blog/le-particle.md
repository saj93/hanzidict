了 (*le*) appears constantly in Mandarin, and learners are often told it marks the past tense. That's not quite right — and the misconception causes confusion for years.

了 doesn't mark tense. Chinese doesn't have grammatical tense the way English does. What 了 marks is **completion** or **change** — and those two uses are distinct enough to treat separately.

---

## Two Different 了

了 appears in two positions, and each position has a different job:

**Verb 了** — immediately after the verb. Marks that the action is complete.

**Sentence 了** — at the end of the sentence (or clause). Marks a change of state or a currently relevant new situation.

The two can appear in the same sentence. They can also appear independently. Understanding each one separately is more useful than trying to find a single definition that covers both.

---

## Verb 了: Completed Action

了 placed right after a verb signals that the action was completed:

> 我吃**了**饭。*Wǒ chī le fàn.* — I ate (the meal). / I've eaten.
> 她买**了**一件毛衣。*Tā mǎi le yī jiàn máoyī.* — She bought a sweater.
> 他喝**了**三杯咖啡。*Tā hē le sān bēi kāfēi.* — He drank three cups of coffee.

This is **not the same as past tense**. You can use verb 了 about future completed actions:

> 等你吃**了**饭，我们就走。*Děng nǐ chī le fàn, wǒmen jiù zǒu.* — Once you've finished eating, we'll go.

The meal hasn't happened yet, but 了 marks that it will be complete before you leave. Verb 了 is about completion, not past time.

---

## Sentence 了: Change or New Situation

了 at the end of a sentence (sentence-final 了) indicates that a state has changed, or that the current situation is new or now relevant:

> 春天**了**！*Chūntiān le!* — It's spring! (it wasn't before)
> 我明白**了**。*Wǒ míngbai le.* — I understand now. (I didn't before)
> 他不喝酒**了**。*Tā bù hē jiǔ le.* — He doesn't drink anymore. (he used to)
> 饭好**了**。*Fàn hǎo le.* — The food is ready. (it wasn't before)

The sentence-final 了 doesn't describe a completed action — it describes a new state. "He doesn't drink anymore" is about a current state (not drinking) that is new relative to before.

---

## Both 了 Together

A sentence can have both:

> 他吃**了**三碗饭，吃不下**了**。*Tā chī le sān wǎn fàn, chī bù xià le.*
> He ate three bowls of rice and can't eat any more.

The first 了 (verb 了): the eating is complete.
The second 了 (sentence-final 了): now there's a new state — he's full.

---

## Negating 了

To negate a completed action, use **没有** (*méiyǒu*) or **没** before the verb, and drop 了:

> 我吃了饭。→ 我**没**吃饭。*Wǒ méi chī fàn.* — I didn't eat.
> ✗ 我不吃了饭。

Using 不 to negate a completed action is a common mistake. 不 negates habitual or intentional actions; 没 negates completion.

One exception: 不…了 (not verb 了, but sentence-final 了) means "not doing something anymore" or "stopping":

> 我不喝了。*Wǒ bù hē le.* — I'm not drinking anymore / I'll stop now.

This is sentence-final 了 marking a change — previously drinking, now stopping.

---

## When to Omit 了

了 is not required after every past-tense verb. Many sentences describing past events work fine without it — and adding 了 where it doesn't belong sounds unnatural.

你今天做什么了？is fine. But:

> 我昨天去图书馆。*Wǒ zuótiān qù túshūguǎn.* — I went to the library yesterday.

No 了 needed — 昨天 (yesterday) already situates the event in the past, and there's no emphasis on completion or change. Adding 了 here would suggest you're emphasising something about the going, or marking it as the last item in a sequence.

The general principle: use 了 when completion or change is the point of what you're saying. Don't add it mechanically to every past-tense verb.

---

## Duration With 了

Verb 了 with a duration expression indicates how long an action was performed, often with the implication that it's ongoing or still relevant:

> 我学了三年中文**了**。*Wǒ xué le sān nián zhōngwén le.* — I've been studying Chinese for three years.

The first 了 (verb 了) marks the studying period; the second 了 (sentence-final) marks the current state — still studying now.

Without the second 了:

> 我学了三年中文。*Wǒ xué le sān nián zhōngwén.* — I studied Chinese for three years. (completed, done)

The difference is subtle but real: with two 了, you're still at it; with one, you've finished.

---

## A Practical Summary

| Usage | Position | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Verb 了 | After verb | Action is complete | 吃了饭 *chī le fàn* |
| Sentence 了 | End of sentence | New state / change | 明白了 *míngbai le* |
| 不…了 | End of sentence | Stopping / no longer | 不喝了 *bù hē le* |
| Two 了 | Both positions | Ongoing since completion | 学了三年了 *xué le sān nián le* |

了 is worth paying attention to as you listen and read — not to memorise rules, but to notice how native speakers use it. The pattern becomes intuitive much faster through exposure than through grammar study.
